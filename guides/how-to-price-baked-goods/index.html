How to Price Baked Goods: a 6-Step Formula

"What does the bakery down the road charge?" is a fine sanity check, but a bad starting point. Their costs, rent and hours are not yours. Here is the method I use as a professional cook to price from the real cost up.

You need three numbers you probably already have: what you paid for ingredients, how many pieces a batch really gives you, and how long the batch takes you. Everything else follows.

Step 1. Ingredient cost for one batch

Work from what you actually paid per pack, not from memory. For each ingredient: pack price ÷ pack size × amount used. A 2 kg bag of flour at 3.00 is 0.0015 per gram, so 280 g costs 0.42.

Two details that change the result more than people expect:

  • Usable yield. Strawberries lose their hulls, oranges their peel. If only 85% of what you buy ends up in the bake, divide the cost by 0.85.
  • Frostings and fillings. Cost your buttercream once as its own recipe, then add the share each cupcake uses.

Step 2. Packaging per piece

Bags, boxes, cupcake liners, labels, ribbon, stickers. A box that holds six cookies adds one sixth of its price to each cookie. Packaging is small per piece and easy to forget, which is exactly why it quietly eats margin.

Step 3. Divide by the pieces you can sell

A recipe that "makes 24" rarely gives 24 sellable pieces. Some break, a few are a bit too dark, one goes to tasting, two become samples at the market. Divide every batch cost by what you can actually sell. If you lose 5% of 24, that is 22 pieces, not 24.

Step 4. Your time

This is the step most home bakers skip. Count the whole batch: mixing, baking, cooling, decorating, packing and cleaning up. Then:

Time cost per piece = minutes ÷ 60 × hourly rate ÷ sellable pieces

Choose an hourly rate you would accept for any other job. If your time is not in the cost, a price that looks profitable can still mean working for free.

Step 5. Overhead

Oven and mixer energy, a share of your permit or registration, insurance, card fees, market stall fees, the website. Estimate a monthly total, divide by the batches you bake in a month, and add that to each batch.

Step 6. Add your margin

Add up steps 1 to 5 to get the true cost per piece. Then set the price so that the margin you want is left over:

Price = true cost per piece ÷ (1 − margin)

Margin and markup are not the same thing. A 35% markup means cost × 1.35. A 35% margin means 35% of the price is profit, which needs cost ÷ 0.65. The second gives a higher price, and it is the number that matches what you keep.

Worked example: chocolate chip cookies

ItemBatchPer cookie (22 sellable)
Ingredients9.000.41
Packaging—0.15
Time: 60 min at 18/hour18.000.82
Overhead1.500.07
True cost1.45
Price at 35% margin: 1.45 ÷ 0.652.22

The recipe makes 24, the baker loses 5%, so the batch is spread over 22 cookies. Rounded up, that is 2.25 each or 27.00 a dozen. Without the time line, the "cost" would have been 0.63, and pricing at 1.50 would have felt generous. It would have paid nothing for an hour of work.

Common mistakes

  • Pricing from the recipe yield instead of the pieces you can sell.
  • Leaving out your time because "it's a hobby that pays for itself".
  • Using markup and thinking it is margin.
  • Not updating prices when butter or eggs go up. Re-check your costs every few months.
  • Copying a local price without checking it covers your own cost.

What margin should I use?

There is no single right number. Start from a target, compare the resulting price with what customers in your area pay for similar bakes, and adjust. If the price is far above the local market, look at your costs first: a smaller box, a cheaper supplier or a faster method often fixes it without cutting your pay.
